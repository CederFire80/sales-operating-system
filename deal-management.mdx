---
title: "Managing deals, and forecasting them honestly"
description: "Stages defined by customer actions, a consistent qualification toolkit, a built compelling event, and a forecast you can defend."
---

A pipeline is only useful if everyone reads it the same way and the leader can stand behind the number. I get there by defining stages around what the customer actually does, qualifying with a consistent toolkit, building a reason for the deal to close now, and forecasting from what I can defend.

## Why it matters

Forecasting gets reliable the moment every stage means the same thing to everyone. Anchor each stage to a customer action and the roll-up becomes a shared, honest picture, which is what lets a leader coach the deals that need it and commit a number the whole team can trust.

<Note>
  A stage advances when the customer takes an action. That is what makes the pipeline honest.
</Note>

## The stages, by customer action (**Illustrative**)

| Stage | Customer action |
| --- | --- |
| Qualify | Signed an NDA |
| Sandbox | Opened a sandbox |
| Evaluate | Live evaluation started |
| Commit | Economic buyer at the table |
| Close | Agreement signed |

## The method

<Steps>
  <Step title="Define every stage by a customer action">
    Tie each stage to something the customer did: signed an NDA, opened a sandbox, started a live evaluation, brought the economic buyer to the table. Objective stages make the pipeline mean the same thing to everyone, and they turn forecasting into reading facts.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Qualify with a consistent toolkit">
    Run MEDDPICC on every deal so the gaps are visible early, use SPIN on first calls to get from a problem to its real implications, and press the five whys to a clear "why act now." For the competitive deals, arm each rep with the one thing you do better, stated in a single sentence.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Build the compelling event">
    When a deal has no clock, give it one. Map a transition plan backward from the date the customer needs to be live, so the decision has to happen now to make the timeline, and offer a clean head-to-head so the choice is made on evidence. Urgency you build on the customer's own timeline is urgency that holds.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Forecast from what you can defend">
    Trust the booked base, inspect the tail, and commit the number you can stand behind in the room. Give the hardest look to the oldest deals and the ones riding on a single large opportunity, and ask for stage, age, and MEDDPICC on each, plus win rates by rep and by source. A clean disqualify is a win: it makes the number you commit one you can trust.
  </Step>
</Steps>

## The compelling event, in practice

<Tip>
  On a replacement deal with no urgency, I worked backward from the date the customer wanted the new system live and built the transition around it: a quarter to enable, a quarter running both systems side by side, a quarter fully switched with the old one as backup. Laid out that way, the decision had to be made now, a full year ahead, to hit their own date. I paired it with an open comparison against the incumbent, let the evaluation run, and the choice got made on evidence. It closed months faster than a deal like that usually does.
</Tip>

## What good looks like

- The pipeline reads the same to everyone, because a stage means the same thing to everyone.
- The forecast holds, because it is built on customer actions and inspected where it is thin.
- Deals carry a reason to close now, grounded in the customer's own timeline.
- The leader's time goes to the few gates where judgment and network change the outcome.
